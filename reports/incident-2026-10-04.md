# Incident, 4 October 2026, when the upstream stopped sending

This is the one failure in the project that nobody staged. The replay reached the end of the
held-out period, the feeder had nothing left to publish, the customer export went stale, and
the scoring job refused seven nights running while an alert sat open in Cloud Monitoring.

Everything below was read back out of Cloud Logging and the Monitoring API on 12 October,
after the fact. None of it was written at the time.

## What the system was supposed to do

`reports/replay.md` was written before any of this happened, and says what the end of the
replay would look like:

> When the held-out period runs out the feeder publishes nothing and says so, rather than
> looping back to the start. **The scoring job will refuse on staleness within 24 hours,
> which is what should happen when an upstream stops sending.**

The alert policy's own runbook text, written on 21 September, says the same thing:

> The nightly call-list job did not publish a list. The most likely cause is a stale or
> missing customer export: the freshness gate refuses rather than publishing yesterday's
> list as though it were today's.

So the prediction is on the record with a date on it, twelve days before the event.

## What the system actually did

### 1. The feeder ran out, and said so

Vertex job `5120046044163342336`, 3 October 19:01Z, which is 4 October 01:51 Asia/Bangkok:

```
replay source: the held-out period, 8,239 rows the model never trained on
the replay is exhausted: no export was published.
The scoring job will refuse on staleness within 24 hours, which is what should happen
when an upstream stops sending.
The replica workerpool0-0 exited with a non-zero status of 2.
```

It did not loop back to the start of the campaign, and it did not rewrite the export with
the last day's rows. It published nothing and exited non-zero.

### 2. The scorer refused, ten minutes after the feeder was scheduled

Vertex job `8952609327055634432`, 3 October 19:02Z, which is 4 October 02:02 Asia/Bangkok:

```
fetched gs://itcs355-6688010/capstone/incoming/contacts.csv (51,562 bytes)
STALE: input is 24.2h old (modified 2026-10-02 18:52Z, limit 24h)

STALE INPUT — published nothing.
A missing call list is noticed in minutes. A plausible wrong one is worked through for a day.
The replica workerpool0-0 exited with a non-zero status of 2.
```

**Exit 2 is the stale-input path** in `scripts/score_nightly.py`, the branch that returns 2
after emitting `published=0` and `refused=1`. The same exit code appears in the demo
(`python scripts/run_demo.py`, scene `stale`), where the staleness is manufactured. Here it
is real: the file in the bucket genuinely had not been rewritten.

Note what the job did *not* do. The export was structurally perfect. It was a real 600-row
slice that had passed the contract the night before. Scoring it would have produced a
plausible, well-formed, completely wrong call list: last night's customers, published as
though they were tonight's. The gate is second in the pipeline, before the contract, so the
file was refused for being old rather than passing every structural check on the way to
being used.

Seven clean 24-hour steps. The job did not get stuck, crash differently, or start ignoring
the file. It woke up every night, measured the same object's timestamp, found it one day
older, and refused again. A control that fires once can be luck. A control that produces
that column is working.

## Why Vertex shows twenty-one FAILED jobs

Anyone opening Vertex AI → Custom jobs sees a clean cut: thirty-six consecutive SUCCEEDED
runs from 21 September to 2 October, then **twenty-one consecutive FAILED**, seven nights of
feeder, scorer and measure, and nothing after. The FAILED runs are the refusals.

This is a deliberate choice and it has a cost worth naming. The scorer exits non-zero when it
refuses, because an orchestrator that is told "fine" has no reason to stop, retry, or
escalate. The price is that **Vertex cannot tell a refusal from a crash**, because both are
`JOB_STATE_FAILED`, so the job state is not the signal. The signal is the metric.
`published=0` with `refused=1` means a control fired. A crash emits neither, because the
process is gone before the emit. That is why the alert watches `published < 1` rather than
job status.

The retries in the logs are Vertex's own. Each night's refusal appears about five times,
which is why the `published` series has five zeros per night rather than one. Each retry
reached the same conclusion for the same reason.

## What this actually proves, and what it does not

**Proved.** The gate refused a structurally valid file for being old. The refusal was
measured, not just logged. The metric crossed the threshold, the policy opened an incident,
and the incident was delivered by email. The system then held that position unattended for a
week without anyone touching it.

**Not proved: routing.** The alert went to one personal inbox. There is no rotation, no
severity on the policy, nothing that pages anyone, and no second recipient. The honest test
of a 3am alert is whether someone would have acted on it, and the answer here is visible in
the evidence itself. The incident stayed open for seven nights, so nobody did. Detection and
delivery work. Getting it to a human who is awake is not built.

**Not an outage, either.** Nothing was broken. The replay was always going to end: 8,239
held-out rows at 600 a night is fourteen nights, and it ran twelve scheduled nights plus two
forced ones. The correct behaviour at the end of a finite replay is exactly this, to stop
publishing and complain. The alternative, looping back to the start of the campaign,
would have produced a system that published a fresh-looking list forever while measuring its
own lift against rows the model had already been trained on.

## The twelve nights before it

The run the incident interrupted, for context. Realised lift is the published list's
subscription rate against the whole export's. Because the replay already knows every
outcome, `scripts/measure_yesterday.py` runs at 02:10, ten minutes after the list was
published, rather than waiting weeks for the calls to come back.

| Call list | Realised lift |
|---|--:|
| `call-list-2026-09-21.csv` | 1.241 |
| `call-list-2026-09-22.csv` | 0.577 |
| `call-list-2026-09-23.csv` | 1.000 |
| `call-list-2026-09-24.csv` | 1.420 |
| `call-list-2026-09-25.csv` | 1.195 |
| `call-list-2026-09-26.csv` | 1.241 |
| `call-list-2026-09-27.csv` | 1.631 |
| `call-list-2026-09-28.csv` | 1.352 |
| `call-list-2026-09-29.csv` | 1.307 |
| `call-list-2026-09-30.csv` | 1.338 |
| `call-list-2026-10-01.csv` | 1.552 |
| `call-list-2026-10-02.csv` | 1.496 |

Those are the twelve objects in `gs://itcs355-6688010/capstone/call-lists/`, and each row
was read from the `realised_lift` series paired with the `call list for <date>` line the
measure job logged.

Twelve scheduled nights: **mean 1.279, sd 0.279, standard error 0.081**. Including the two
forced runs on 21 September: fourteen measurements, **mean 1.294, sd 0.260, standard error
0.069**.

The gate admitted the model on an estimated **1.34×**. The fourteen measured nights sit
0.046 below that, about two thirds of one standard error, which is to say the held-out
estimate and the realised outcome cannot be told apart at this sample size. That is the
useful result: a held-out score is a prediction about the future, and this one was checked
against what happened rather than filed.

One night came in at **0.577**, below 1.0, meaning the ranked list did worse than calling
the same number of people at random. One night in twelve. It is in the series above and in
the dashboard, and it is the reason the gate is a decision about a distribution rather than
about a number.

## Current state

The three scheduled jobs were **paused** on 10 October 13:16Z, before the presentation,
rather than deleted, so the evidence above was still readable afterwards. Everything in this
report was then read back out of Cloud Logging and the Monitoring API on 12 October, with the
incident still open and the policy still enabled.

**On 12 October 06:31Z the whole deployment was torn down**, because the course requires it.
The schedulers, the dashboard, the alert policy and the capstone image are gone, and
[`teardown-check.json`](teardown-check.json) records an empty `still_running` list. So the
console no longer shows any of this, which is exactly why the two definitions it was read
from were committed first:

- [`alert-policy.json`](alert-policy.json) holds the policy as the Monitoring API returned
  it, with the `published < 1` condition, the `0s` duration, the attached notification
  channel and the runbook text quoted at the top of this report.
- [`monitoring-dashboard.json`](monitoring-dashboard.json) holds the six panels. Four
  describe the run and two describe the result. A dashboard of inputs can say the job ran,
  and only those two can say it helped.

Restarting the replay would mean extending its source, not resetting
`capstone/state/replay-cursor.json`. Neither is done here, because a replay reset that reuses
rows the model trained on would make every number after it meaningless.
